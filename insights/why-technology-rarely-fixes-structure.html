Why technology rarely fixes a structural problem

Article, 2 min read, by Paul Thallner

A firm I spoke with had just finished a long, expensive software rollout meant to solve a coordination problem between two of its practice groups. The tool was good and the implementation was clean. Eighteen months later the two groups were still working separately, now inside a very capable platform.

When a firm feels the strain of its own structure, it tends to reach for something it knows how to buy: a new system, another offsite, a chief of staff, a redesigned bonus pool. Each is something a firm can purchase and point to, and each lets everyone feel the problem is being handled.

Sometimes it is. Often the operating model underneath all of it stays the same.

The tool inherits the structure

Technology does not decide who owns a decision. It does not tell two practice groups how to share a client, or settle whose targets matter when they conflict. It encodes whatever logic the firm already has, so unclear logic becomes faster and better documented. The offsite generates energy with nowhere to go on Monday. The new bonus pool rewards behavior the structure still works against.

A question about how the firm is built can't be answered with a purchase.

Good tools, good offsites, and thoughtful compensation all have their place, and sequence decides how much they return. They compound when they sit on top of a clear operating model, and they disappoint when they are asked to stand in for one. The firm that maps its decision rights first gets far more out of the system it buys second.

Before the next investment, ask whether it solves a structural problem or decorates one. If the model underneath has not changed, the tool will not change it either.

The structural work, once done, makes everything bought afterward worth more.
