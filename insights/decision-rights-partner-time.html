Decision rights are the quiet tax on partner time

Article, 2 min read, by Paul Thallner

Ask a partner where last week went and the answer often includes very little client work. The time went to a thread that needed three people to weigh in, a decision that came back around because someone who should have been consulted wasn't, and a meeting held to decide who decides.

That time is a tax. It rarely shows up on any report, and a growing firm pays it every week.

Decision rights answer a plain question: for any given decision, who owns it, who needs to be consulted, and who only needs to be told. In a small firm, everyone knows the answer without writing it down. The founders decide, everyone else can watch them do it, and the firm runs on shared memory.

Shared memory stops working as the firm grows. Add partners, offices, and practice areas, and the unwritten map gets crowded. Two people each believe they own the same call. A third assumes someone else has it. The decision gets made slowly, and slowness in a partnership is paid for in partner attention.

What unclear ownership costs

Delay is the visible cost, and the larger one comes after it. Work routes around the ambiguity. People stop raising issues, because raising them starts a process nobody enjoys. The partners with the most goodwill absorb the friction, which hides the problem until one of them decides they have had enough.

Good judgment needs a clear owner before it can do the firm any good.

Mapping decision rights is unglamorous work, which is part of why firms put it off. It means naming who owns the calls that have always lived in a gray zone, and it surfaces disagreements that were easier to leave buried. The firms that do it get something back quickly: decisions that move, partners who spend their hours on work only they can do, and accountability that belongs to a role.

The aim is a small number of clear answers to the decisions that matter most, held in a form the firm can maintain. Get those right, and much of the friction partners attribute to people turns out to have been a structural question all along.
